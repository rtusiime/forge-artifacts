How to Decouple Math (Without Becoming Alpha)

A proposal from Kevin · August 2026 · Sources · ~15 min read

Back in April we asked ourselves a question: if we teach math inside challenges, out of textbook order, are we "breaking some law of physics"? Are kids missing steps we can't even see, because we learned this stuff so long ago? This month we got the answer, three different ways: a worksheet, a lesson that broke, and a spreadsheet column. Here's a plan for what to do about it. It keeps everything that makes Forge Forge.

The short version

  • Math is the one subject where every skill sits on top of the one before it. Challenges are great at making kids want the math. They're a bad way to teach the skills in the right order.
  • The plan: one protected hour a day where every kid practices the next skill on their own list, writing on paper. Challenges then use math the kids already have.
  • Each skill gets taught once. No more teaching ratios twice in two different challenges, and way fewer lesson plans to write.
  • A person, not a screen, stands behind every skill: kids work on paper every day, and a guide reads that paper. Five-minute whiteboard chats happen every couple of days, sooner if the paper looks off. And nobody tutors a skill without passing the chat on it first.

Part 1 of 5: Why Now: Three Signals in One Week

  1. Signal one: the worksheet. On Friday, a strong student working above grade level tried four problems. He got 23/40 = 0.575 exactly right, using a clever trick: he worked out 1/40 first, then multiplied. Then he used the same trick on 5/7 and wrote 0.710. As an exact answer, that's wrong. 5/7 is 0.714285…, and the pattern repeats forever. When he was asked about it afterward, the story got more interesting: he could tell the decimal kept going. He just had no name for that, no way to write it, and no rule for when to stop. School never taught him any of it; the little he knew came from test prep on the side.
    • So he can divide. But "divide a small number by a bigger one, and handle a decimal that never ends" is a 7th grade standard (7.NS.A.2.d). That's two grades above him. He needed it anyway, and nobody knew it was missing.
    • And the ladder keeps going, and it's the state's ladder, not ours: turning a repeating decimal back into a fraction is grade 8 (8.NS.A.1). Each rung has a code and a year attached.
    • Here's the uncomfortable part: he's one of our strongest kids. So "most of our kids are ahead" doesn't protect us. Strong kids hit this wall too, and they hit it quietly, feeling sure they're right.
  2. Signal two: one of our own lessons broke on the same skill. That same week, we caught a problem in the St. Hubert's fraction lesson (1.C.0). The lesson assumed kids could divide 4 by 5 and get 0.8. Our incoming 5th graders can't do that yet. We caught it in review before any kid saw it, and we double-checked with a friend who teaches 6th grade math. And here's the thing: this slipped past all of us, on a team that knows math well. That's what an expert blind spot is. We learned this stuff so long ago that we can't see the steps anymore.
    • Even Illustrative Math has the same blind spot. In April we looked at their own chart of what-comes-before-what, and it doesn't list fractions before ratios or percentages. None of us believed that.
  3. Signal three: the dupe column. We now keep a "potential dupe" column in the tracker, because different challenges keep teaching the same math without knowing it. Surveying got taught in Uniforms and again in Big Dog. Ratios got taught in Uniforms and again in St. Hubert's, a few weeks apart. Every dupe is a lesson somebody wrote twice, reviewed twice, and taught twice. And this is the month we're trying to write about 96 lessons.
    • The dupe column treats the symptom. The cause is that every challenge has to teach its own math. If challenges just use math from one shared track, teaching something twice becomes impossible. We won't need the column at all.
  4. The real problem underneath all three. We want five things at once:
    Fast kid acceleratesnever waits for the room
    Behind kid catches upquietly, no spotlight
    Mixed agesone community, 5th–8th
    Lean guide teamfour guides, no hires
    Minimal screenspen and paper firstthe one we bend, honestly

    In a normal classroom, where the whole room moves at one pace, you can't have all five. One pace means the fast kid sits and waits, and the kid who's behind struggles in front of everyone. I've tried for months to design around this. I don't think it can be done with one-pace teaching.

    • This plan keeps four of the five exactly as they are, and bends the screens one honestly: kids do every problem on paper, showing their work. The app's only jobs are to pick the next problem, schedule the reviews, and find the missing skill behind a wrong answer. Here is what that last one means. A kid keeps getting 1/2 + 1/3 wrong. The real trouble is usually not adding at all: he cannot turn halves and thirds into sixths. The app notices that the wrong answers all share that one older skill, sends him back to practice it, then brings the addition back. That's the write-it-out version of tech we've said we actually like.

Part 2 of 5: The Daily Frontier Block

  1. Find each kid's frontier. Each kid takes a short diagnostic. It gives them problems that get harder, one skill at a time (fractions, then division, then decimals, and so on), and it stops when the answers stop being right. Everything up to that point is math the kid can already do without help. The first skill they miss is their frontier, and it's the one thing they practice. It's the Friday worksheet, given to everyone, once. After that, the daily work keeps it up to date on its own.
    • Results stay between the kid and the guides. The frontier list is for teaching. It's not a leaderboard, and it never goes on a wall.
    • So far, only one student has tried the worksheet, and he's a strong one. The next step, which we already agreed on: give the same four problems to a typical rising 5th grader. Then we'll know what our real incoming students can and can't do, not just what one strong kid can do.
  2. Set aside one protected hour, and note what goes in it: two independent blocks, not one. Kids work on the vocabulary they are picking up across the challenges, and they work on their own math. Both are the same kind of thing — the sort of learning that only comes from steady independent practice over weeks, and that a challenge lesson cannot deliver no matter how well it is written. This is not a math-only fix. Everything else already exists. Our proposed daily schedule already has a peer tutoring slot and a catch-up slot in the afternoon. The only change is setting aside one protected math hour in the 9:45–12:30 morning. One block moves. Nothing else changes.
    • The tech stays small and easy to run: laptops come out for this one hour, locked to the one math app, no open internet. All the actual math happens on paper. If a laptop dies, the kid keeps working on paper and nothing falls apart.
  3. Pick the app that runs the ladder.
    WHO DOES WHAT
    The app teaches most of the skills and decides when each kid practises them again. The guides read the paper, work out what actually broke, explain it a different way when it does not land, and tie it back to the real thing the kids are building.
    The block needs the things the research actually supports: don't move on until a skill is solid, bring skills back for review before kids forget them, mix up the practice, show worked examples. And when a kid gets something wrong, find the missing skill behind it (the halves-into-sixths problem from earlier) and fix that one. Math Academy is the app that fits. It covers the whole 5 through 8 range, schedules every review on its own, and when a kid misses a problem it points at the missing skill behind it. Its algorithm decides what each kid sees next, and under this plan that is fine. Here is what the app actually does. When a kid reaches a skill they have never seen, the app teaches it: a short lesson, an example worked all the way through, then problems to try. After that the app keeps giving practice, and it brings the skill back weeks later so it doesn't fade. (A few ideas get introduced inside a challenge instead of the app. Part 4 covers those.) The challenges run on their own calendar no matter what the app does.*
    * We also checked Beast Academy, which lets teachers control what opens when. It is out: its content stops around 5th grade, and we run 5 through 8.
    • One rule that's not negotiable: kids write everything on paper and show their work. The screen just shows the problem and checks the answer. No multiple choice, and no staring at a screen all day. Paper is the usual way, not a gate: a kid who struggles to write can build it, draw it, point, or talk it through. The reasoning has to be visible. The pen is optional.
    • The best evidence here isn't test scores. It's an old, famous finding (Bloom, 1984): kids who got one-on-one tutoring didn't just do better. They stopped spreading apart. Normal classrooms are what create the huge gap between the top kid and the bottom kid. Letting each kid move at their own pace shrinks that gap. That's our biggest math worry, kids at totally different levels in one room, fixed at the source. One honest caveat: Bloom found this with human tutors. We're not assuming an app plus peer coaching captures all of it. That's one of the things the pilot measures.

Part 3 of 5: People Check What the App Can't

  1. Guides go where the app can't see. During the hour, every kid is in one of three groups for today's skill: green (the app thinks they've got it), yellow (close, still practicing), or red (stuck, because an earlier skill is missing). Guides spend their time in two places. With the kids who are stuck: sit down one-on-one, find the missing earlier skill, teach it. And at the whiteboard (more on that below), checking with kids the app says are done, to make sure the skill is real. And if the whole room goes red on the same skill, that's the signal to teach one regular whole-class lesson on it. Now we actually know when those lessons are needed.
    • Why do people need to check at all? Because the app can only check one small skill at a time. There are two things it can never see: can the kid use their skills on a big messy problem nobody set up for them, and can the kid explain their thinking to another person. A green checkmark tells you neither. People check what the app can't.
    • This is also our coaching promise made real. Our coaching page tells families "your coach knows your strengths, your struggles, your goals" and "tracks your progress over time." The frontier list and the whiteboard chats are exactly how that happens for math: each kid's coach can open their regular one-on-one with the actual ladder, the rungs cleared this month, and what's next. The promise gets a mechanism.
    • One honest note: the colors are our classroom shorthand, not the app's screen. The app tracks each kid its own way (a skill map and points). The guides translate that into green, yellow, and red for the room. And the colors describe today's status on one skill, never the child. Nobody gets called a red. The list lives with the guides.
  2. Yellow kids get peer tutors. And mixed ages means we have tutors on day one. A kid earns the tutor seat at the whiteboard: they can tutor a skill only after passing the whiteboard chat on it with a guide, solidly, not barely. Green on the app alone is never enough. Once earned, they tutor the kids who are yellow on that skill. This is the Montessori thing we've talked about since April: students teach each other, and the guide steps in only for the big gaps. The usual worry is that at the start of the year, nobody's green yet. In our school, that's not true. The 7th and 8th graders walk in already strong on the skills the 5th and 6th graders are working on. Their confirming chats take five minutes each, so the tutor bench fills within the first days.
    • There's a built-in safety net: a guide reads every kid's paper daily, and the next whiteboard chat is never more than a couple of days away. So if the tutoring didn't stick, we find out at the very next check, and nobody slips through.
    • The tutor's chair is also where kids learn to explain their thinking. As the tutor, you're the one who knows the thing, helping someone who doesn't. That's the good kind of pressure. It's practice at presenting your ideas, not being put on the spot.
  3. The whiteboard chat: five minutes, no grades, every couple of days. Why bother, if the app already says green? Because the app can be fooled. Remember the worksheet: that student got a "right" answer using a memorized fact, without ever doing the actual division. The app sees a right answer and moves on. A person catches it with one question. So before a green counts, the kid does one problem at a little whiteboard with a guide, showing their steps.
    • The paper catches the trick. The chat explains it. When kids show their work, a shortcut is sitting right there on the page. What the chat adds is the why. One friendly question, like "cool, does that decimal ever stop? how do you know?", tells you whether the kid made a rounding choice or is missing the idea completely. Those two need different fixes.
    • Put the strictness in the task, not in the grilling. If today's skill is long division, the card says "show it with long division." Now the shortcut just isn't an option. And shortcuts fall apart on the next problem anyway. The memorized-1/7 trick stops working the moment you hand the kid 4 ÷ 13. That's how you tell a trick from a real method.
    • Keep it small and friendly, on purpose. Even a gentle one-on-one check can make a kid feel judged, and a kid who feels judged stops showing you their real thinking. So chat results get used for exactly two things: deciding what to teach next, and unlocking the tutor role. They never go in a grade book, and kids never get ranked.
    • One thing to watch for: some kids know the math but have a hard time explaining it out loud. If that seems to be what's happening, the guide lets them show it another way, like a quick drawing or a worked example. And if it's clear they know the math, we don't re-teach the math. We coach them on explaining their thinking, and they're great candidates for the presentation moments in challenges, where that skill gets real practice.

Part 4 of 5: What Challenges Look Like After

  1. Challenges use math the kids already have. The "I need this math" moment stays. The re-teaching goes. Designing a challenge changes from "where do we squeeze ratios in?" to "where does this challenge really need a skill some kids have?" Everyone works on the same real question (which section supports uniforms more?), but each kid contributes at their own level. One kid turns the tallies into fractions. One compares 5/8 to 6/12. One takes 5/7 all the way into decimals and reports why it never ends.
    • No labels, no tiers, nothing called "the 8th grade problem." The harder version just sits in the same problem set, and any kid can choose to try it. The guide knows who to quietly nudge toward it.
    • The challenge is also where kids get to use their skills on a big messy problem nobody set up for them. That's one of the things the app can never check, and here it happens in real work, in front of a guide.
    • And skills still come back around. Every new challenge revisits old skills as new kids reach them. The only thing that dies is scheduling the same first-teach twice. A guide re-explaining something a kid needs said differently is normal teaching, and that stays.
    • One rule keeps the records honest: finishing a challenge never counts as proof that a kid mastered the math inside it. A kid who used a helper tool or supplied numbers participated fully, and that's good. But mastery gets shown in one place only: the block, checked by a guide. Nobody has to qualify to join a challenge, and nobody gets marked as "knows it" just because their team finished.
    • One planning habit makes this airtight: every math idea gets a debut home when we plan the year. If a challenge can genuinely introduce it, the way the adoption data introduces what a rate is, it debuts there. The block only introduces the ideas no challenge can carry, and it handles the practice for everything.
    One question, one thread, three rungs. Everyone's math is real, and the rungs check each other.
    GARDENS CHALLENGE · SAME TRICK, DIFFERENT MATH
    One instruction: size the new garden beds.
    1. Bed A is 4 ft by 8 ft. How much space? (multiply the sides. Grade 3 work, 3.MD.C.71)
    2. How much edging board goes around Bed A? (add up all four sides. Grade 4, 4.MD.A.32)
    3. Bed B is 4½ ft by 8¾ ft. How much space? (same question, fraction sides. Grade 5, 5.NF.B.4b3)
    4. Bed C is L-shaped. How much space? (cut it into two rectangles first. Grade 6, 6.G.A.14)
    5. Fill Bed B 8 inches deep. Soil comes in 1.5 cubic-foot bags. How many bags do we buy? (volume, then round up, stores don't sell half bags. Grade 7, 7.G.B.65)
    Five rungs of one strand, and the answers feed each other: the bag count needs the fraction-sides area. Every rung is real work for the garden.
  2. We write way fewer lesson plans. Right now, every math skill inside a challenge means somebody writes a full lesson, gets it reviewed, and often re-writes it. With the block, three things change. The daily practice comes from the app, so nobody writes it. Challenge math becomes short "use it" moments instead of full teach-it lessons. And whole-class math lessons only get written when enough kids need the same thing at the same time.
    • What "the whole class needs one" actually means: say it's October, and over one week, eight different kids all get stuck at long division. The guides can see that pile-up on the frontier list. That's the signal to write and teach one whole-class lesson on long division. It gets taught because the room needs it right now, not because a challenge happened to land on that page of the calendar.
    • And here's the ratios story, told the new way. This year we wrote a full teach-ratios lesson for Uniforms: hours of writing, plus review, plus re-writes. A few weeks later St. Hubert's needed ratios, and we started teaching it all over again. With the block, nobody writes a whole-room teach-ratios lesson again. Kids meet ratios in the block when they reach that rung, with practice the app generates. The few kids who reach that rung right when a challenge needs it get a fifteen-minute small-group lesson timed to that exact moment, and that is the only ratios lesson anyone ever schedules. Then Uniforms needs half a page: take the vote tallies, turn them into ratios. St. Hubert's needs half a page: take the adoption numbers, do the same. Two half-pages instead of two full lessons, and the second one isn't a re-teach. It's just more practice on a skill they already own.

Part 5 of 5: The Ask

One reversible decision

Build the fall math around a daily block, instead of inside the challenges. Here's what that means this month:

  • I write the fall math as short "use it" moments that assume the block exists. This changes how I write lessons this week, and it's less work, not more.
  • We give the four-problem check to a typical rising 5th grader, and we give everyone the diagnostic at orientation.
  • We run the block for the first four weeks of school: one hour, paper-first, laptops locked to the math app, and the app's leaderboard and league features turned off, since we don't rank kids. Then we look at three numbers. First: skills cleared per kid. A skill counts as cleared when a kid practices it in the block on paper, goes green in the app, and a guide reads that paper. Chats every couple of days spot-check cleared skills, and tutoring a skill always takes passing a chat on it first. That's one rung up the ladder. Every kid, fast or behind, should be climbing steadily, and a kid sitting on the same rung for two weeks is a flag for the guides. Second: how many whiteboard chats got passed. Third: how much guide time still goes to math rescue inside challenges. That last one should head toward zero. And one slower check: two weeks after a skill is cleared, we spot-check it with fresh numbers. Cleared should stay cleared. If it doesn't, our chats are getting too easy.
  • And one thing to decide together, not decided here: how the block's progress data feeds each student's 1:1 coaching check-ins, and whether some whiteboard chats should sit next to those check-ins for the kids who need the gentlest version. The block and coaching should feel like one system to a kid, with one boundary kept: the coach reads the ladder and celebrates it; the check itself stays a routine moment in the math hour, so the coaching relationship never turns into an audit.
  • If the numbers say it isn't working, we put math back inside the challenges. We've lost four weeks of one hour a day, and we keep everything we learned about each kid either way.

Anticipated Questions

Q: We built this school on the idea that the challenge creates the need for the math. Doesn't this take that away?
A: The opposite, and it is worth separating three different things people mean by that word.

Wanting to settle the question. Which section really supported uniforms more? Why do big dogs sit in the shelter longer? That is created by the challenge, for the whole room, and nothing here touches it. The opening of our own uniforms lesson does this beautifully already.

Wanting a particular skill. This is the one that changes, and it changes for the better. Today the challenge creates the want and then satisfies it eleven minutes later by teaching that skill to everyone, which means only the kids in the middle ever feel it. The kid who learned it two years ago feels nothing. The kid who is not ready feels defeated, which is not the same as motivated. Under this plan the want gets created the same way and satisfied on that kid's own clock. And it gets created on purpose rather than by luck: every lesson models two versions of the work, so every kid watches the version one step above his own get done, out loud, by an adult or a classmate.

Wanting to keep climbing. This is the one the app cannot do for itself. Points and streaks are thin motivation. The reason to do your hour is that you saw somebody settle the vote with a number you could not produce yet. And when your own ladder reaches that skill, the guide closes the loop in one sentence: this is the thing you needed for the vote.

The honest cost: for some kids, weeks pass between wanting the skill and getting it. We think that is a better trade than today, where the wanting is instantly satisfied for a third of the room and never happens at all for the rest. We are not removing the felt need. We are removing the assumption that thirty-five kids feel it on the same Tuesday.
Q: What do we say to a parent at an open house who hears "an hour on a computer"?
A: Say this: your child does not learn math from a screen. They learn it from a person and from paper. For one hour a day, a program picks the next problem at exactly their level, the way a private tutor would, and your child solves it on paper with a pencil, showing every step. A teacher reads that paper every day and sits down with your child face to face every couple of days. The program never grades them, never ranks them, and never decides what they know. It just makes sure that when your child sits down, the problem in front of them is the right one for them, instead of the right one for the middle of the room.
Q: Isn't this just Alpha?
A: We're taking the one thing Alpha actually got right, which is letting each kid move at their own pace in math, and leaving the rest. At Alpha, the app is the school and the projects are dessert. Here, the challenge stays the whole point of school. The block just exists so the challenges work better: kids stop hitting math walls in the middle of them, and guides stop doing emergency arithmetic instead of coaching. A person confirms every skill. Kids teach kids. Work happens on paper. Alpha's app replaces teachers. Ours hands teachers better information.
Q: We promised families minimal screens.
A: And we keep that promise. One hour, laptops locked to one math app, no open internet, all the real work on paper. The app only does the three things paper can't do for 35 kids at once: pick each kid's next problem, schedule the reviews, and find the missing skill behind a wrong answer. The rest of the day looks exactly like it does now. And there's a quiet bonus: this is the private way to catch a kid up. Their catch-up work happens on their own list, at their own desk. Nobody else ever knows. The other way to catch a kid up happens in front of the whole room.
Q: If the app says green, why do we still need people checking?
A: The app checks one small skill at a time. There are two things it can't check: can the kid use their skills on a messy real problem, and can they explain their thinking to someone else. A green checkmark says neither. Also, a right answer isn't proof. A memorized shortcut can produce right answers without the real skill, and the app can't tell the difference. A person with a whiteboard can, in five minutes. The paper shows the method, the chat finds the why, and the challenge is where kids put it all to work.
Q: Doesn't the challenge lose its power if it isn't teaching the math?
A: The challenge keeps its two best jobs: making kids want the math, and giving them a real place to use it. It drops its worst job: teaching skills in a strict order, on a fixed calendar, to kids who are all in different places. We've now seen twice what happens when a challenge is forced to be the math teacher. Either the math bends the challenge out of shape, or a hidden missing skill snaps under it. That's the 1.C.0 story. Take the teaching out, and the challenge's own story actually gets simpler and better.
Q: Why can't a challenge teach the practice part, if we design it really well?
A: Because of how long practice takes, and this number isn't ours. New Jersey teaches division by strategies in grade 5 (5.NBT.B.6) and only expects it automatic, "with accuracy and efficiency," in grade 6 (6.NS.B.2). Same story for decimals: strategies in grade 5 (5.NBT.B.7), automatic in grade 6 (6.NS.B.3). The state budgets a full year between "can do it" and "does it without thinking." The federal research guide says the same thing in different words: for learning to stick, students need to meet the material again weeks to months later. A challenge is one window of a few weeks, pointed at one problem. You can introduce a skill there, and you can use a skill there. You can't fit a year of spaced practice inside it. Same reason one four-hour workout doesn't make you fit.
Q: Going forward, couldn't we just design challenges so the math shows up in the right order?
A: Partly, and we should. New challenges can absolutely be designed with the math ladder in mind, and the current ones aren't really movable anyway. But a perfect lineup only fixes ordering, and ordering was only half the problem. First, our kids are spread out: at any point in the year, some kids hold skills that others don't, and a calendar can't fix a difference that exists between kids at every moment. Second, the practice problem doesn't care about order: no arrangement of challenge windows adds up to a year of spaced practice on one skill. Third, challenges collide: in our own fall plan, Gardens needs scale drawings, scale drawings need proportional reasoning, and the challenge that would teach proportional reasoning is St. Hubert's, which runs at the same time. We found that by checking the prerequisite map, not by eyeballing the plans.
Q: Won't more and more of math end up in the block over time?
A: No, and the standards themselves keep the list short. Across grades 5 through 8, only a handful of skills are flagged for automatic-level fluency: multiplying multi-digit numbers (5.NBT.B.5), long division (6.NS.B.2), decimal operations (6.NS.B.3), and in grade 7, solving simple equations fluently (7.EE.B.4a). That short list, plus the fraction work underneath it, is the block's whole non-negotiable core. Everything else in math is a concept or an application, and concepts can still make their first appearance inside a challenge, where wanting-to-know does its best work.
Q: Can four guides actually staff this?
A: This is the easiest hour of the day to staff, because most of the helping isn't done by guides. Kids who are green on a skill coach the kids still working on it, and with mixed ages, those coaches exist from day one. The official check stays with the guides, and the numbers work: a kid finishes a skill maybe two or three times a week, not every day. Across 35 kids, that's roughly 15 to 25 short chats a day, split across four guides, pulled from a queue as kids become ready. The app keeps the middle of the room moving. The guides focus on two things: the stuck kids, and the skills where a right answer often hides a wrong understanding (this fractions-to-decimals cluster is the perfect example). And one nice thing: the whiteboard chats and the peer tutoring aren't two separate programs to run. They're the same activity. The kids who passed the check are the ones giving it.

What Changes On Our Own Documents

The short version: it is not less work. It is different work. We stop writing the same skill lesson twice for two age groups, and we stop teaching a skill to twenty kids when four of them need it. What we write instead is one set of challenge tasks that has real work at every level in the room.

Here is what that means for the three challenges we have already built, by name.

Uniforms, Act 1. Two lessons run this same moment, and reading them side by side is the clearest picture of what changes. The 5/6 lesson, What Fraction of Us?, spends its middle half teaching one math skill to the whole room: comparing fractions with different bottom numbers, modeled with bars, coached in pairs, then practiced without the card. It finds out at minute eight, with a whiteboard check, who is missing the skill underneath it, and its own instructions say to schedule a separate re-teach later. The 7/8 lesson, Spreadsheet Basics on Our Own Vote, does something completely different: it teaches a tool. Kids build a live sheet on their own room's vote, lock the total, and watch every percent move when one count changes. Its own aim calls the percent math "review-in-use," deliberately not new, and when a kid cannot do percents by hand it says the formulas do the computing for now and flags that kid for later.

That second lesson is already what every lesson looks like after this change. The first one is what they look like before it.

So, concretely: Spreadsheet Basics survives almost untouched. The only thing it loses is its opening percent check, because we would already know who can do percents by hand. Everything else in it is exactly right: the math is review, and the lesson teaches something the app cannot, a tool and a habit of checking your own output. What Fraction of Us? keeps its best parts, and they are genuinely the best parts of the document: the silent prediction, the two counts, the moment where "more than half" cannot separate the two sections, the stuck question written on the board, and the reveal at the end where kids settle the real vote. What it loses is the twenty-five minutes in the middle where one skill is taught to everybody at once.

Here is the part that matters, because we have a rule about it: we never ask a kid to do something alone that nobody showed them first. That rule does not bend. The showing moves to the hour. The app teaches each skill with a worked example when that kid arrives at it, and a guide checks it before it counts. So on challenge day, every kid is working with math somebody already taught them and already checked.

Which means the lesson still opens with the guide showing something. It just stops being the arithmetic. The guide models the judgment instead: what counts as the whole here, ballots in the box or votes that counted; how you check whether a comparison is fair; how you know when your answer is precise enough to argue with. That is the thinking no app teaches, it is the same for every kid in the room whatever math they have, and it is what the room is for.

Then the guide shows the work itself, and here is the rule that keeps this from turning into ten lessons at once: two versions per room, and the guide models both. In the 5/6 room that might be settling it with fractions and settling it with percents. In the 7/8 room, settling it with percents and settling it with a display that survives someone checking it. Four versions across the school, not thirty-five. Both get modeled out loud, front of room, on practice numbers, about five minutes each: the same time the old lesson spent modeling one.

Two things fall out of that, and both are the point. Every kid has now watched somebody do the version he is about to do, so our rule holds. And every kid has also watched the version one step above his, which is the moment a kid decides he wants that. The guard: the guide is showing how the math gets used here, not teaching the math itself. If a kid cannot follow the harder version, nothing is wrong. It was not his to do today.

Then the work: each kid takes the version his own math reaches, and a kid whose math reaches neither gets the finished number printed on his page and still makes the call. Nobody gets a task using math nobody taught him, and nobody sits through a lesson he finished two years ago.

St. Hubert's, and the pattern across four lessons. We read four finished lessons side by side, two from each challenge, and they sort into three piles.

Pile one, survives untouched. Spreadsheet Basics (7/8, Uniforms). Its math is already review by design. It teaches a tool and a habit, which is exactly what a lesson should teach once the skills live somewhere else.

Pile two, dissolves into the hour. 1.C.0 Fraction Fluency Review (5/6, St. Hubert's). The name says it: a review lesson for dividing a part by a whole. That is precisely what daily practice at each kid's own level does better, every day, instead of once for everyone.

Pile three, splits in two. What Fraction of Us? and 1.C.1 Percentage Versus Number. Look at what 1.C.1 actually contains. Half of it is a procedure: write the fraction, simplify it, divide, read it out of 100. That half is the app's job, on each kid's own clock. The other half is the idea the challenge exists for: Madison adopted 30 big dogs and North Branch adopted 10, but Madison had 120 waiting and North Branch had 20, so North Branch's ten beats Madison's thirty. A raw count can lie. That half is worth a room full of kids arguing, and here is the important part: a fifth grader who cannot compute a single percent can still see that ten out of twenty is half and thirty out of a hundred and twenty is a quarter. The judgment does not require the procedure. Today those two halves are welded into one 45-minute lesson, so the kids who already own the procedure sit through it, and the kids who cannot hold it yet get locked out of the argument.

Gardens. Same shape, with the beds: multiply the sides, add up the edging, handle the half-foot measurements, cut the L-shaped bed into rectangles, work out how many bags of soil to buy. Five levels, one job, and the bag count needs the answer from the fraction-sides step, so the levels feed each other rather than sitting side by side.

One more thing that only shows up when you read what comes next. After What Fraction of Us?, the 5/6 room's next lesson is Say It as a Ratio. After Spreadsheet Basics, the 7/8 room's next lesson is Chart Crimes, where kids take apart the same sheet they built and see how a true set of numbers can be drawn to lie. Look at what decides the order in each room. In the 7/8 room, the next lesson comes from the work: you built a chart, so now find out whether your chart is honest. In the 5/6 room, the next lesson comes from the math: you did fractions, so ratios come next. And the lesson itself says so out loud. What Fraction of Us? has a line instructing the guide that ratio words are not used today, because they belong to the next lesson. The vote did not ask us to hold that vocabulary back for a day. A teaching order did.

That is the order-of-math versus order-of-the-work problem, sitting inside our own Act 1, in two documents, in the same week. Be fair about the strength of it: ratio language is genuinely useful for describing a school-wide vote, so this is a tell rather than a proof, and worth checking before leaning on it. But the direction is clear enough. Once the skills live in the hour, the younger room's week is free to be ordered by the vote, the way the older room's week already is. We are not proposing something foreign. We are proposing that the younger room's week look like the older room's week already does.

What we stop making: the from-scratch skill lesson for a skill the app already teaches, and the second teaching of a skill in a later challenge (we wrote a full ratios lesson for Uniforms, then started writing ratios again for St. Hubert's). Note what we do NOT stop making: a different lesson for a different age group when it is genuinely a different lesson, the way the spreadsheet lesson is. What we start making: one set of tasks per challenge with a rung for every kid, and a small stack of already-solved example problems copied from the curriculum we already own, kept on the table for kids who learned the skill recently. What we keep making, unchanged: the challenge itself, the opening that creates the need, the decision, the artifact, the public presentation.

What Forge Actually Needs

THE WHOLE THING, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
Forge needs a math track that runs on its own clock instead of the challenge calendar. It has to do five things: find out where each kid actually is, teach them the next thing they are missing, keep bringing old skills back so they stick, check weeks later that they still hold, and let a kid who is ahead keep moving while a kid who is behind catches up, at the same time, in the same room.

That is the requirement. Notice what it does not say: it does not name an app. An app is one way to build that track. It is not the only way, and the point of writing the requirement down first is so we argue about which engine to use instead of arguing about whether we need one.

This is what keeps the challenges short

Read this part as protecting the challenges, not raiding them. Right now Act 1 of Uniforms gives four lessons to work that both published curricula spend eight to ten on. There are only two ways to close a gap like that. Either something outside the challenge teaches the skills, or the missing lessons go inside the challenge.

Do the second and watch what happens to the calendar. A three-week act becomes six. Six becomes ten. St. Hubert's was never meant to run at the same time as Uniforms, so either it does now, or it slides — and everything behind it slides too. That is not a small cost. It is the challenge calendar we designed, gone.

And here is the part that makes it worse. Even after all that, it might still not work. Ten lessons in a row on ratios is not the same as ten lessons spread over months. The reason a skill sticks is that it comes back after a gap. The government's own practice guide on this says to bring key material back at least twice, separated by weeks to months. A challenge cannot do that to itself. It ends.

So the honest comparison is not "with a track" against "without one." It is:

  • With it: challenges stay the length we designed them, and the skills get built somewhere that can space them out properly.
  • Without it: challenges get much longer, the calendar collapses into itself, and we still probably do not get kids fluent — because you cannot squeeze months of spaced practice into one challenge no matter how many lessons you add.

That second row is the thing to sit with. We would be paying the whole cost and might not even get the thing we paid for.

Helping a kid finish today's work is not the same as teaching him the skill.

This one sentence is where every argument we have had about this actually lives. Both lists below are real work. We do the first list well. The second list is not on anybody's plate.

THIS IS WHAT WE DO NOW
Gets a kid through today's task
  • Give him a calculator
  • Give him a spreadsheet formula
  • Leave a worked example on the table
  • Use easier numbers
  • Hand him the finished number
All five are fine. All five should stay.
NOBODY IS DOING THIS
Makes him able to do it himself next month
  • Find out what he already knows
  • Teach the missing piece underneath
  • Watch him do it, then let him do it alone
  • Bring it back weeks later so it does not fade
  • Check a month on that he can still do it cold
Not one of these five is anybody's job right now.

Doing the left list well, for years, produces a kid who can take part in every challenge and still cannot do the math. That is the whole worry, and it is not an argument against scaffolding. It is an argument that scaffolding was never the thing that teaches.

Said another way: the challenge's Tuesday is not the kid's Tuesday.

Four things we all call "scaffolding," and they are not the same thing

This word has been doing too much work in our conversations. Four different moves have been hiding inside it, and they have different consequences.

The moveIs the work still real?Does he end up able to do it?
Teach it on easy numbers, then use the real onesYesMaybe — only if he keeps practising it afterwards
Swap the real data for made-up easy dataNo — this is the one that costs us the challengeHe might learn the steps
Let him use a calculator on the real dataYes, fullyNo. The calculator does that part, every time
Hand him the finished numberYes for the decision he is makingNo. Nothing here builds the missing piece

Two things fall out of this. Only the second row damages the challenge — and it is the one that has actually been happening, because when a lesson gets generated the easy fix is to invent tidy numbers. Made-up numbers are the real complaint, not scaffolding in general. And three of the four rows are honest ways to get a kid through today while leaving him exactly where he was. That is not an argument against any of them. It is the reason something else has to be doing the teaching.

There are only so many ways to do this. Here they are, honestly, including ours.

Why "guide hours" is doing more work than it looks like in row one. On August 5 the arithmetic was set out for us: 120 lessons, 96 still to write, 16 build days — 1.5 finished lessons per guide per day, with evenings and weekends named as the fallback if we slip. And 120 is only the number that gets us through September. A full semester is closer to four hundred.

Here is where that stands. As of August 13, with 27 days to launch, the review log holds about 78 lessons. 24 are marked Ready. Thirteen are Close, thirteen are in Rework, and roughly two dozen have been submitted and not yet reviewed. That is the pace against the September number, before anyone writes the other three hundred.

Row one asks these same people, in these same weeks, to also test every kid, sort them into groups, re-sort those groups as kids move, and write different material for each group. There is no slack to take it from. Row one is not expensive. In this term, it is not available.

Given what we actually have — four guides, two grades in a room, kids arriving all over the place, no appetite for ranking children in public, and a challenge order we want to keep — the second row is the strongest candidate. Not proven. Strongest candidate. That is exactly why the ask is a pilot with agreed ways to fail, and not a purchase.

The Evidence That This Costs Something Later

The strongest evidence here is not ours and it is not about our kids. In 2012 a team led by Robert Siegler followed two large groups of children, one British and one American, from elementary school into high school. What they found: how well a child knew fractions and division in elementary school predicted their algebra and overall math achievement five to six years later — and it still predicted it after the researchers statistically removed the effects of the child's other math knowledge, their general intellectual ability, their working memory, and their family's income and education.

Read that carefully, because it is a narrower and stronger claim than "math builds on itself." Two children with the same general ability, the same memory, the same family circumstances, and the same overall math scores — the one who knew fractions and division better in elementary school did better in algebra six years later. That is the specific piece of math this challenge spends, and it is the piece with the longest shadow.

It does not tell us how many lessons anything takes. It does tell us that this particular strand is not a place to be casual, and that the cost of getting it wrong is not paid this term.

How We Would Know This Is Wrong

A pilot nobody can fail is not a pilot. Here is what we would accept as proof that this is the wrong idea, agreed before we start.

First, be clear about what any one test can kill. This plan is answering four separate problems. Evidence about one of them says nothing about the other three.

  1. Kids are in different places. One lesson fits almost nobody.
  2. We write a lesson for every skill, from scratch, for every group.
  3. The order math has to be learned in and the order challenges need it in are not the same order. One of them always loses. Right now it is the challenge.
  4. Time. New Jersey starts strategy-based division and decimal work in Grade 5 and expects it accurate and efficient in Grade 6 — development stretched across grades, not weeks. A challenge lasts six to ten. One challenge cannot carry a skill from nothing to solid.

Problems three and four are structural. No pilot result makes them go away. A pilot only tells us whether the thing we build handles them.

Before we spend a single hour, the diagnostic can kill it. The whole argument rests on kids being scattered. If the diagnostic at orientation comes back and most of the school is within a couple of skills of each other, then one lesson taught to the whole room was the right tool all along, and we should not run this at all. That check costs us one morning and it can end the debate before it costs anything else.

The test that can prove either side wrong. A placement check tells us how spread out the kids are. It does not tell us whether four lessons can do the job, which is the thing we actually disagree about. So: run a short probe on the exact skills this block needs, teach the four lessons as written with the real vote numbers and a calculator rule decided in advance, then check three things — did they get it that day, can they still do it a week later, and how many guide-minutes it cost. Then give them a different voting problem they have never seen, a week later, with no help. Agree the pass mark before anyone looks.

Here is how each of us loses. If kids can do the fresh problem a week later on their own, four lessons were enough and I was wrong to worry. If they can only do it while the support is sitting in front of them, then the support solved taking part and did nothing for learning. If the guide-minutes come back huge, the plan where each guide fixes their own kids' gaps has failed on staffing even if it is right on teaching.

Then, at ten weeks, any one of these means it is not working:

  • The same lesson keeps being needed by most of the room. If the group that needs the same explanation on the same week is regularly more than half the class, we have added bookkeeping to reinvent the whole-class lesson. That is a loss, and we should say so.
  • Cleared skills do not stay cleared. We spot-check old skills with fresh numbers. If kids who passed a check two weeks ago cannot do it now, our checks were too easy and "cleared" means nothing.
  • Kids are not moving. If the typical kid is not clearing skills at a steady rate, the hour is not buying the thing we bought it for.
  • The face-to-face checks stop happening. If a big share of kids go more than a few days without a real conversation with a guide, the staffing math was wrong, and the whole model rests on those conversations.
  • Kids get parked in challenges. If the kids furthest behind are quietly contributing nothing to the real work, the tasks were not built wide enough, and the promise that nobody waits outside the room was not kept.
  • The culture leaks. If kids start asking each other what level they are on, we have built the ranking we said we would not build.

And if it fails, we are not left with nothing. Each way this can fail points at a different next move. Naming them now keeps "this isn't working" from turning into a shrug.

  • The same lesson keeps being needed by most of the room. Per-kid ladders bought us nothing. Go to the version we costed out in July: stop treating challenges as places to apply math taught elsewhere, and make the challenges themselves the math curriculum. The price is that the order we run challenges in stops being a story decision and becomes set by what has to be learned before what.
  • Kids are not moving. The app was the wrong engine. Fall back to guide-led groups, three per room, re-sorted every couple of weeks. Same idea, no software.
  • Cleared skills do not stay cleared. Our checks were too easy. That is a repair, not a verdict.
  • The face-to-face checks stop happening. The staffing math was wrong. Either fewer and deeper checks, or more of the load carried by reading the daily paper.
  • Kids get parked in challenges. We cut the work too narrow. Widen it, or fix the planning row for that challenge.
  • Kids start comparing levels out loud. Culture leak. The lists go fully private and we audit how adults are talking.
  • The diagnostic says kids are mostly at their own grade level. Then we do not need the app or a separate ladder per child. But we still need work at several levels, because our rooms hold two grades each and a school-wide challenge spans four. So: write each task at a couple of grade levels and hand a kid the one matching his grade. Same idea, far lighter bookkeeping. That is the honest cheap version.

Nothing we have found puts today's model back on the table intact.

The hour is one block. That is the whole reason to try it this way.

Things That Could Go Wrong (and How We Keep Them From Happening)

  • The block's rules leak out of the block. In the math hour, "prove it, pass it, move up" is the whole game, and that's fine there. The leak is when that thinking shows up in challenge time. It sounds like this: "You can't work on the adoption-rate table until you're green on ratios." Or kids asking each other "what rung are you on?" when teams are forming. Or a kid having to qualify before they're allowed to present. None of that is allowed. In a challenge, every kid works on the real question with whatever math they have, and nobody has to earn their way in. That's the mistake Alpha actually made: their whole school became one long "prove you're ready." Ours keeps it to one hour.
  • Chats turn into grades. Whiteboard results decide what to teach next and who can tutor. The moment they become scores or rankings, the friendly check becomes a test, and we lose the exact kids we're trying to help quietly.
  • We misread the quiet kid. "Can't explain it on the spot" means one of two things: they don't get it, or they get it and can't find the words. Opposite problems, opposite fixes. So the guide always gives them another way to show it before re-teaching anything.
  • The tech grows. One app, one hour, locked laptops. If it grows past that, we've traded one classroom-management problem for another.
  • We start trusting the dashboard too much. The dashboard is a guess. The daily chat is what keeps it honest. If we skip the chats when we're busy, green quietly stops meaning anything.
