The Decision Playbook

How to decide well when the data is cheap, and when it isn't. Good judgment without the reading list.

v0 · July 2026Companion to: The Lesson Audit PlaybookFor: the Forge team
The whole thing in one move

Before you argue about a decision, sort it. Is it reversible and cheap to test, or not? That one fork decides which tool you reach for. Most bad meetings are people applying slow, careful, one-way-door thinking to a decision they could just test in an afternoon, or waving through a one-way door on a gut feeling. Sort first, then argue.

Step 1 · Which door is it?

Two-way door

Cheap to reverse, low cost if wrong, happens often. (Which lesson format. Which prompt. Which skill layout.)

Stop arguing. Test it. Go to Step 2.

One-way door

Expensive or impossible to undo, or a rare outcome that is catastrophic. (What you promise a parent. A safety call. Shipping 700 lessons.)

Slow down. Reason it through. Go to Step 3. Do not wait for a body count.

Step 2 · Two-way doors: be empirical, honestly

Empiricism wins here, but only if the test is honest. A sloppy test is worse than good judgment, because it dresses a hunch up as a number and gives it authority.

  • Run the cheapest real test that could actually move you: an A/B, a small pilot, a proxy. If one exists, run it before the meeting.
  • Pre-register. Before you look, write down what you predict and the exact result that would change your mind. Skip this and you will "notice" whatever you already believed.
  • Blind it if a human scores it. Strip the labels. You will favor the option you expect to win.
  • Watch the tail, not the average. One safety miss outweighs ten prettier documents. A better median with a worse worst-case is not better.
  • Goodhart's warning: the moment a proxy becomes the target, it starts drifting from the goal. Re-check the proxy against the real thing now and then.

Step 3 · One-way doors: the ladder

When the clean test is too slow (does this curriculum actually teach a kid, over a year), climb this in order. Most "untestable" decisions fall off the ladder before the top.

  1. Shorten the loop. Find a leading indicator: a fast proxy that sits upstream of the slow outcome (exit-ticket mastery, retrieval three weeks out, transfer to the Bridge). Validate it against the real thing once, then trust it.
  2. Make it reversible. Version it, keep a rollback, build a fast correction loop (the maintainer model, the teach-prep read). If you can observe reality and cheaply fix it, you have turned the one-way door into a two-way door. Reversibility substitutes for evidence.
  3. Borrow the experiment, but know what you are borrowing (Step 4).
  4. Test it cheaply. Teach the lesson to three kids and watch where they stall. Run a pre-mortem (assume it failed a year out, write down why, fix those now). Red-team it. None of these prove the outcome; all of them surface the biggest failure modes this week.
  5. Calibrated judgment, for the residue. Write the prediction with a confidence number, find the base rate for decisions like this, and score yourself when reality arrives. Judgment improves only if you keep score. Untracked intuition never gets better.

Step 4 · The borrowing rule (the sharp one)

"Someone already ran the study, so we inherit it" is the most powerful move on the ladder and the easiest to get wrong. There are two very different borrows:

Borrow a mechanism ✓

How people learn: explicit instruction, worked examples, spaced retrieval, the testing effect. Independently replicated across many settings, robust to how you implement it, about human cognition. Strong. Borrow freely. (Rosenshine holds because he built it from three independent lines of evidence, not one.)

Borrow a track record ✗

"This product raises scores, so our version of it is good." Implementation-dependent, tested at a fidelity nobody actually runs, and adoption is driven by marketing and politics as much as results. Weak. Never lean on it.

The test of "settled" is independent replication, not consensus. Consensus can be everyone copying the same wrong number. Physicists crept toward the true charge of the electron for years because they threw out results that disagreed with the famous first measurement, rather than trusting their own. (Feynman, Cargo Cult Science: "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool.")

The Forge cut

Grounding a lesson in Illustrative Math buys factual correctness and non-fabrication (strong: it is our defense against invented knowledge). It does not buy inherited teaching quality (weak: we rework it, so their efficacy studies protect no one, us included). And sometimes a mature source's real value is neither: it is raw material. IM was the forcing function that produced our own standards graph. Borrow content for correctness, borrow mechanisms for method, use artifacts as launch pads, but never outsource your quality argument to someone else's pedigree.

Run this before you litigate

  1. Two-way door or one-way? Reversible, cheap, frequent means test it and stop arguing.
  2. What is the cheapest test that would move me? If one exists, run it before the meeting, not in it.
  3. What would change my mind, written down, now? No pre-set threshold means ego decides later.
  4. Am I borrowing a mechanism (fine) or a track record (careful)?
  5. What is the tail? Insure the median and the rare catastrophe. Ignore the merely annoying.
