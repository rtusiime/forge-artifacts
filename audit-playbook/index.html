FORGE PREP

The Lesson Audit Playbook

How we stand behind ~700 AI-written lessons without reading 700 lessons.

For: The 7-8 lead · the 5-6 lead · Kevin · The new guide (day one friendly, start at Section 3) v2.3 · July 16, 2026 Owner: Kevin
Read this first · the 30-second version

We have about 700 AI-written lesson plans and we can't read them all. We can't hit 100% fidelity, so this playbook is built to catch the two things that actually wreck a lesson: a kid learns something wrong, or the lesson can't run because it leans on something that doesn't exist yet. Everything below is that one goal, drawn as an assembly line.

In one breath: scripts check what's checkable on all 700 (sources real, numbers compute, prerequisites in order, safety flagged); people spend their four minutes only where judgment lives; the load-bearing fifth of lessons get cross-examined by a second AI; and every bug gets filed on the doc, never said out loud. This playbook's owner is Kevin. Found a problem with it? Leave an AUDIT: comment and tag Kevin.

Section 0 · The Big Idea

Why we audit, in plain English

Our lessons are written by AI that we point at real, battle-tested curricula: Eureka, Illustrative Math, OpenSciEd. That grounding is the base of our quality argument, but be precise about what it buys. Pointing a lesson at a real source gives it factual correctness and keeps the AI from inventing knowledge. It does not inherit the source's teaching quality, because we rework every source, and a published curriculum's results depend on being run the way it was studied, which nobody does. Grounding stops fabrication. The audit and the people earn the rest. The audit exists to answer one question, and the question is not "is this lesson good?" Nobody can answer that 700 times. The question is:

The audit question

"Did anything get into this lesson that we can't stand behind in front of a kid, a parent, or the reviewer?"

That question splits into two kinds of checking, and the whole system below is this one sentence drawn as a flowchart:

The one principle

Software checks the checkable. People judge the judgment calls. A script can verify that a cited source exists, that answers compute, and that lesson 12 doesn't depend on something built in lesson 30. Only a person can tell whether something is worth teaching, whether it would survive a seminar, and whether the reviewer would wince. Never spend human minutes on what a script can verify, and never trust a script with anything that needs taste.

We learned the second half of that the hard way. In June, a reading that passed every readability number got torn apart in review because it had been simplified into mush: technically grade-level, nothing in it worth discussing. The metric was right and the human was right. They were measuring different things. That is why every gate below has a script part and a human part.

Section 1 · Know Your Enemy

The six ways an AI lesson goes wrong

Every one of these has already happened to us at least once. That's why it's the list.

The 5-6 lead's compression · the three buckets

If six modes is a lot to hold at once, they collapse into three questions. Is the content correct? (invented knowledge, fake grounding, a wrong load-bearing word). Can we actually do it at the right grade? (wrong grade band). Are the prerequisites in place? (broken timeline, the "depends on something not taught yet" bucket). Safety sits above all three and outranks everything.

  • 0. Safety. Outranks everything.
    Anything where a kid touches the physical world: soil, chemicals, food, tools, heat. This is the one category where "good enough for v1" is banned. A bad worksheet costs a re-teach. A safety miss can cost something we don't get back.
    Caught by: a keyword scan that routes the lesson to a named person for sign-off. The script flags, only a human clears. The garden soil question is the live example, and it needs a written decision before any hands touch ground.
  • 1. Invented knowledge.
    The AI teaches a concept that doesn't exist. It happened to us: a survey lesson confidently taught a category of question that no textbook anywhere contains. Kevin went looking for a source and found nothing, because there isn't one. This is the worst failure class. A kid walks away knowing a fact that isn't a fact.
    Caught by: the cross-examination in Section 4, plus question 2 of the human read in Section 3.
  • 2. Fake grounding.
    The lesson claims it's built from a real source but barely used it. The citation is decoration. Kevin checked one lesson against the Eureka lesson it cited and found it only half matched. A separate session once ignored our own OpenSciEd library and searched the web instead. If the grounding is fake, the quality we think we're inheriting isn't there.
    Caught by: a script that confirms every cited source actually exists (in our library or a vetted outside source), plus a spot comparison during the deep pass. Note: changing the theme is fine. A dog lesson built from a cow lesson is the product working. See Section 9.
  • 3. Broken timeline.
    A lesson depends on something that doesn't exist yet. On Monday we caught the 7/8 track analyzing the 5/6 cohort's survey results two acts before the 5/6 kids run that survey. Same idea at the math level: a lesson needs a skill whose prerequisites nothing on the calendar has taught yet.
    Caught by: the prerequisite checker (already built, see Gate 1) for the math layer, a small script for the artifact layer, and question 5 of the human read.
  • 4. Wrong grade band.
    The content is real but pitched at the wrong kids. Monday's example: a 7/8 lesson whose graphing work was all 6th grade level, reading bar charts when those kids should be analyzing two-variable data. The June readings are the other direction: leveled down so hard they stopped being worth reading.
    Caught by: readability numbers and standards lookups as a first pass, human taste as the final word. No metric ever gets the verdict by itself.
  • 5. One wrong load-bearing word.
    The lesson is right but a key term is off, and a teacher running it as-is would teach the wrong idea. Monday's example: a hypothesis lesson that kept calling a prediction a bet. Small on the page, big in a kid's head.
    Caught by: the teach-prep read (Gate 4), on purpose. Whoever preps the lesson is exactly the person who can fix a word live. We don't spend July minutes pre-hunting these.
Section 2 · The Assembly Line

The flowchart: every lesson rides this exact conveyor

Read top to bottom. A lesson only moves down when it passes a gate. Anything that fails routes right, into the challenge maintainer's fix queue (Section 5). It never quietly stays on the belt.

⚙️ A LESSON IS GENERATED (Claude + skill + cited sources, about 10 minutes)

FAIL → maintainer fix queue

GATE 1 🤖 Automatic checks. Every lesson, zero human minutes.

WHO: scripts (Kevin builds and owns) · WHEN: right after generation · COST: none
  • Sources are real. Every lesson ends with a Sources and AI Grounding Table (the generator skill already writes one). The script reads it and confirms each listed source actually exists: a real, reachable citation, whether it's from our library or a vetted outside source. No dead or invented citations. (Not restricted to our library, per the 5-6 lead: her Big Dog lesson leaned on a shelter-intake page for context. Outside sources are allowed, but they get tagged for a human to vet, so we catch link rot without knocking out real grounding.) BUILD, half day
  • The grounding table is accurate. A quick scratch-check (the maintainer now, a script later): does the Sources and AI Grounding Table actually match what the lesson used? A table that lists a source the lesson never touched is fake grounding wearing a citation. The deeper "did it use the source well?" faithfulness check lives in Section 4, question 6. BUILD / human check
  • From-scratch rule. If the grounding table is empty, or says the lesson came from model knowledge with no source, the lesson has nothing to inherit quality from. It automatically gets the full Section 4 cross-examination, whatever the subject. Ungrounded means guilty until proven innocent. SAME BUILD
  • Numbers compute. Worked answers in math problem sets actually equal what the problems say. This is a deterministic script doing real arithmetic, not an AI judging math, and that distinction is the point (the 5-6 lead): AI still miscounts, so until that changes every math check is either scripted or confirmed by a person. We never take math on a model's word. SAME BUILD
  • Prerequisites come first (math). The map project already does this: it knows which math skills sit under which, and its checker walks every chain in the challenge slate and prints any skill a challenge needs that nothing on the calendar has taught by then. It ran on July 7 and found twelve of these. Each finding is one Fluency Lane, scheduled on purpose, instead of an emergency re-teach in November. BUILT, wire it to lesson tags
  • Artifacts come first. The same idea for non-math things a lesson leans on: survey results, a prior act's product, a field trip. A small script over the scope and sequence catches the Monday bug (using survey data two acts before the survey exists). BUILD, half day
  • Hazard flag. A keyword scan (soil, chemical, food, tool, heat, glass) routes the lesson to a named person for a safety sign-off. The script flags. Only a human clears. BUILD, one hour

Until the missing scripts exist, question 5 in the human read covers the gap by eye. Once built, they run on all 700 forever, for free.

🔴 EGREGIOUS → maintainer fix queue

GATE 2 👀 The 4-Minute Read. Every lesson, human eyes. LIVE TODAY

WHO: any guide, the new guide included, day one · WHAT: New Material + Exit Ticket ONLY · COST: about 4 min per lesson

This is the 7-8 lead's rule, adopted with two amendments (Section 7). You read the two pages where new knowledge is taught and tested, and nothing else. The full card with the five questions is Section 3. Verdicts: 🔴 Egregious (can't teach it) goes to the fix queue. 🟡 Small (one wrong word) gets a suggestion comment and the lesson proceeds. 🟢 Style gets left alone. It's v1.

GATE 3 🧠 Load-bearing fork: does other math stand on this lesson?

WHO: the challenge maintainer, using the map · COST: about 12 to 15 min, load-bearing lessons only (roughly 1 in 5)

Why this isn't just folded into Gate 1's math check (the 5-6 lead's question): Gate 1 computes, by script, across the whole challenge slate, any prerequisite skill nothing has taught yet. It's a filter, and it runs on every lesson for free. Gate 3 decides, one lesson at a time, whether this one is load-bearing enough to earn the expensive Section 4 deep pass. Same map, two different jobs: Gate 1 computes what's missing, Gate 3 routes what gets the deep read. Compute early, decide late.

  • LOAD-BEARING means later work stands on it: ratios, equations, claim-and-evidence, the skills other skills need. This is now a lookup, not a judgment call. The map shows what each skill unlocks. If a lesson's skill has this year's work sitting on top of it, it's load-bearing. A broken lesson here can't be patched next cycle, because three later lessons already stood on it. These get the deep pass in Section 4.
  • APPLIED means challenge-flavored practice: dog-adoption graphs, uniform surveys. An error here is contained. It surfaces in class and gets fixed in the next cycle at low cost. These skip the deep pass and ride the teacher stream.
pass ↓

✅ READY-TO-TEACH SHELF

The lesson sits in the challenge's book (one Google Doc per challenge, a tab per act). On the shelf means: script-checked, human-read, deep-passed if load-bearing, safety-signed if flagged.

GATE 4 📖 Teach-prep read. The audit that costs nothing. LIVE TODAY

WHO: whoever teaches it · WHEN: the night or week before · COST: zero extra, you were doing this anyway

The person prepping the lesson reads it in full. This is the only full read in the whole system and it happens for free. It's where failure 5 (the wrong load-bearing word) gets caught, on purpose, by exactly the person who can fix it live. Findings become suggestion comments with the maintainer tagged.

🏫 CLASSROOM. The only test that counts.

↻ The feedback loop (this is the maintainer model): anyone who hits a problem, teaching it, prepping it, or reading it, leaves a comment on the lesson doc starting AUDIT: and tags the challenge maintainer. The maintainer triages the queue weekly: accept the suggestion, regenerate the lesson, or reply "known, ship anyway." Bugs don't get discussed in the room. They get filed. That's how three people plus the new guide maintain 700 lessons the way open-source projects maintain an operating system. (Fall upgrade, already sketched: a bot you can tag in the comment that drafts the fix as a suggestion, never an auto-apply. FALL)
Section 3 · The 4-Minute Read

The new guide's card. Print this page and you're an auditor.

Zero context needed. If you can read a lesson plan, you can run this. The glossary at the end of this doc defines every Forge word used below.

🔧 THE 4-MINUTE READ, field cardone lesson · about 4 minutes · the scope has no exceptions
1
Open the lesson doc.

In the challenge's book (one Google Doc per challenge), open your assigned lesson tab. Check the tracker row so two people don't read the same lesson.

2
Read ONLY two sections: New Material and the Exit Ticket.

New Material is where the new idea is taught. The Exit Ticket is the mini-quiz that checks the kids got it. Skip everything else: the warm-up, the practice sets, the formatting. Scripts and the teach-prep read own those. Discipline here is the whole trick. Four minutes times 700 is doable. Fifteen minutes times 700 is not.

3
Answer these five questions:
  1. Could I teach this section as written? If a prepared adult can't follow the teach, a kid can't either.
  2. Is every concept real? Would you bet five dollars that each named term, category, or rule appears in a real textbook? A term you've never heard of and can't find in thirty seconds is a 🔴. This question exists because a lesson once taught an invented category of survey question, confidently.
  3. Does the Exit Ticket test what New Material taught? Not something harder, not something from another lesson. This lesson's objective.
  4. Are the printed answers right? Spot-check any worked example or answer key in these two sections with thirty seconds of arithmetic or reading.
  5. Does it lean on something that doesn't exist yet? Any mention of survey results, data from a later act, or work from another cohort: glance at the scope and sequence and confirm that thing is built before this lesson. This question exists because we once had kids analyzing survey data two acts before the survey.
4
File your verdict. Comment, don't discuss.
🔴 EGREGIOUSCan't be taught: invented concept, wrong subject, impossible timeline, safety issue. Comment AUDIT-RED: plus what's wrong, tag the challenge maintainer. The lesson is off the shelf until fixed.
🟡 SMALLOne wrong word, weak example, misnumbered item. Fix it yourself as a suggestion (never a direct edit) with AUDIT-YEL:, tag the maintainer. The lesson proceeds.
🟢 STYLEWordy, bot-flavored, formatting. Leave it. It's v1, and style is not a bug this summer. No comment needed.
5
Log it and move on.

Mark the tracker row (✅ / 🔴 / 🟡 plus your initials). Four minutes means four minutes. If you're at eight you've drifted out of scope. File what you have and move.

Section 4 · The Deep Pass (load-bearing lessons only)

Cross-examine the AI. Never ask it for a grade.

Load-bearing lessons (roughly 1 in 5, plus every from-scratch lesson with no sources) get one extra step: run the lesson through a different AI than the one that wrote it. Our rule: written by Claude, checked in ChatGPT or Gemini. The 5-6 lead's ChatGPT project is already set up for this. The prompt below is deliberately plain text, not a Claude skill: a skill only runs inside Claude, and the whole point here is to get a second opinion from a different vendor's model, so the audit has to travel as copy-paste text you can drop into ChatGPT or Gemini. LIVE

The one rule that makes it work

Never ask "grade this lesson." We tried it. Everything came back A-minus to A-plus with one polite B. An AI asked for a grade flatters you. An AI asked questions it can fail finds things. Ask only questions with checkable answers.

Paste this, attach the lesson (plus its cited source if handy):

ROLE: You are an expert curriculum developer, evaluator, and auditor.

You are auditing a lesson plan for factual and structural errors. Do NOT evaluate style, and do NOT give an overall grade. Answer each item; be specific; cite the lesson's own text.

1. List every named concept, term, category, or rule this lesson teaches. For each: is it a real, canonical concept? Name one real source that uses it, or say NONE EXISTS.
2. List every factual claim you cannot verify.
3. Does the exit ticket assess exactly what New Material teaches? YES or NO, and why.
4. Recompute every worked answer. List any that are wrong.
5. List every concept this lesson USES but does not teach. For each: would a student who only had the earlier lessons in this sequence know it?
6. If it cites a source (Eureka, IM, OpenSciEd): list at least the 3 biggest differences (more if they matter) between the lesson's treatment and the source's treatment of the same idea. Ignore theme changes (dogs instead of cows). Flag changes to the subject content itself: the math, the science, or whatever discipline the lesson teaches. Do NOT flag structure or teaching approach: the source may follow its own model (Illustrative Math is constructivist), and we teach with Rosenshine, so a different lesson structure is expected, not a defect.

OUTPUT: Return a short report, one block per item above. Label each flag 🔴 (can't teach it) or 🟡 (small), give a one-line reason, and quote the lesson text you're flagging. No opening summary, no overall grade. Keep it to about one page per lesson so the maintainer can skim it.

Anything that comes back NONE EXISTS, NO, or wrong gets filed as a 🔴 or 🟡 per Section 3. Expect false alarms. The maintainer's accept/reject is the filter. Ten noisy flags beat one confident A-minus.

How to run it on 70 lessons without opening 70 chats. Two ways. The manual way: in the ChatGPT project, attach 3 to 5 lessons per chat and ask for one answer table per lesson. Never attach more than 5. The more lessons in one chat, the lazier the checking gets on each, and one skimmed lesson defeats the point. That's about 15 chats per challenge, split between two people in an afternoon. The scripted way, worth it at our volume: a small script sends each lesson through the checker model's API one at a time, same six questions, and collects every answer into one findings sheet for the maintainer. One evening to build, a few dollars to run, and after that every newly generated lesson can get checked automatically. Ask Kevin.

Section 5 · Who Owns What

The maintainer model: one name per challenge, bugs go to the name

This section is the timeless rule. The actual name-to-challenge roster changes every few weeks, so it lives on the standup board, not in this manual (the 5-6 lead's point: a manual should read true a year from now, so nothing that changes belongs in it).

The rule

Every challenge has exactly one maintainer: the single person accountable for that challenge's lessons and its AUDIT: queue. Whoever generated the lessons may or may not be the maintainer. Maintainer is the accountable name either way.

What a maintainer actually does (about 30 to 45 minutes a week, not a second job): triage every AUDIT: comment on your challenge weekly (accept the suggestion, regenerate the lesson, or reply "known, ship anyway"); keep your challenge's book as the single source of truth; make the load-bearing call for your lessons using the map (Gate 3); and you are the only person who resolves a 🔴. Owner means accountable, not "generated it once." Bugs get filed on the doc, never raised out loud in the room. A verbal bug is a lost bug.

Where the live roster lives: the standup board. This is the one thing in the playbook that is not timeless, so it is not kept here. Snapshot as of the July 13 standup (expect it to drift): the 5-6 lead owns 5-6 Big Dog and the AI challenge; the 7-8 lead owns 7-8 Big Dog and Gardens; Kevin owns Uniforms; about two challenges are still unassigned, and one person carries three total. Put names in boxes at standup, not in this file.

Section 7 · Ruling on the Table

The 7-8 lead's proposal: only read the New Material section, for all lessons

Verdict

ADOPTED. It's the right 80/20 instinct and it's now Gate 2, with two amendments that cost one extra minute.

Amendment 1: add the Exit Ticket (one extra page, about a minute). New Material is where invented knowledge lives, so the 7-8 lead's targeting is right. But the exit ticket is the test of the teach. If it quizzes something the lesson never taught, you find out live, in front of kids, at the worst possible moment. Read the teach and the test together. They're a matched pair.

Amendment 2: it only works with the script gate underneath it. The uncomfortable data point: Monday's two real catches were the bet-versus-prediction wording (in the warm-up) and the survey-data timeline bug (in the challenge context). Neither lives in New Material. A pure New-Material-only read, applied to Monday, catches zero of Monday's actual bugs. That's not an argument against the 7-8 lead's rule. It's the reason the conveyor has other stations: the prerequisite checker and the artifact script catch timeline bugs mechanically, and the teach-prep read catches wording drift for free. Human eyes go where judgment lives. Everything else has to be caught by a script or by a moment that already exists.

Net: the 7-8 lead's rule sets the human scope. The system covers what it deliberately skips. Adopt it, defend the 4-minute discipline, and don't let it quietly grow back into full read-throughs.

Section 9 · The Anti-Scope

What we deliberately do not audit (so nobody burns a July afternoon on it)

Not a bugWhy
Theme changes from the source (our lesson is dogs, the source lesson is cows)That's the product working. The rule, the 5-6 lead's, now written down: sources are exemplars we use to develop our content, not content we copy. They give us the subject matter, the mechanics (how a concept is actually explained and worked, for example how photosynthesis gets walked through, or how long division is stepped out) and the real figures, all translated into our challenge's theme and taught our way. They are a starting point we build from, never a template we have to match. Flag changes to the actual subject content. Celebrate changes to the story, and to how we teach it.
Grade placement compared to the source curriculumA published curriculum's sequence reflects that curriculum's model. Forge's own scope and sequence governs. Their placement tells us how far from the mainstream we are. It is information, never a verdict.
Bot-flavored prose, wordiness, formattingv1. A condensed print version is a one-prompt transform later. Style polish is the classic bottomless time sink. Cap it at zero for July.
Perfect readability scoresSee the parable below.
The seminar readings parable (June 2): why no metric ever gets the verdict

Kevin leveled a set of seminar readings with a proper readability tool. Lexile bands, the works. The numbers came back grade-appropriate. Then the readings got torn apart in review, because leveling had chopped the sentences so short that all the complexity was gone, and complexity was the point: these texts exist to be discussed. The tool measured sentence mechanics. The job was ideas. Kevin adjusted his approach on the spot, and the lesson stuck. Every script in this playbook is a floor. The ceiling, "is this worth a child's hour," is always a person. That division of labor is the playbook.
